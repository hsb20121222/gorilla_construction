What we built (and why)

The assessment is based on Ben Finio's Science Buddies guide. Here's our spec sheet — every choice on this list earned its place the hard way:


  • Two vertical loops: One loop is the brief. Two is a statement. They're stepped in height so the marble keeps enough speed to survive the second apex.
  • Two funnels: Inverted paper trapezoids that trade straight-line speed for slow orbital spirals — our secret weapon for stretching run time.
  • Corkscrew spiral: A folded 3D helix that bleeds off speed gently between features instead of losing it all in one drop.
  • Banana basin: The finish line is banana-shaped. There is no engineering reason for this. There doesn't need to be.
  • Self-supporting: Everything stands on an A3 cardboard base held up by straw truss towers. No walls, no hands, no cheating.
the banana was Hyeon-seo's idea. We regret nothing.

Why it's shaped like this

Logan's straw cross-bracing + Hyeon-seo's track geometry (funnels, loops, spirals) + Jayden's height maths. The full lap:


The run: the marble starts as high as the tower allows, because height = stored energy — that's the whole game. It drops into Funnel #1 and orbits while feeding slowly through the hole, regains speed through Loops #1 and #2, threads the corkscrew spiral, takes one last spin in Funnel #2, then rolls to a stop in the banana. Simple to describe. Eleven prototypes to build.

PERFORMANCE & VERIFICATION

Testing: the numbers don't lie

Five measured runs on the final build. The video below is one of them — real footage, real marble, actual screaming (off-camera).

The rebuild nobody saw coming

Our first layout was a disaster: the marble died in funnel #1 about half the time, and when it survived, it never cleared loop #2. Diagnosis (Jayden, armed with a ruler): too much friction in the funnels, not enough starting height. So we rebuilt — raised the tower, re-pitched funnel #1, taped every loop joint smooth, and banked the corkscrew so it feeds straight into funnel #2. Run time went from a lucky 9 seconds to a repeatable 23, and the marble now finishes every single run.

THE SCIENCE BIT

Physics: why the marble behaves

Energy conversions, friction, and the maths that decides whether loop #2 lives or dies. Verified with a ruler, a stopwatch and one very patient marble.

1. Why funnels and spirals stretch the run time

In funnel #1, the marble's straight-line kinetic energy converts into orbital rotation — centripetal force pins it to the funnel wall while gravity feeds it slowly through the exit hole. Funnel #2 does the same job near the finish. The corkscrew spreads the remaining speed loss along a long, gentle helix instead of one sudden drop. Result: 23 seconds of run time from a 45cm start. The funnels are the heroes.

2. The loop #2 problem (energy in action)

A loop needs speed at the top, not the bottom. At the apex, gravity and the track must together supply centripetal force — below a minimum speed, the marble falls. Our starting height is 45cm; loop #2's apex sits at roughly 12cm, so only (45 − 12) = 33cm of height converts into apex speed. Our first build lost too much of that to friction and failed exactly there. Raising the tower and smoothing the joints restored the margin — and now it clears every time.

3. Where the energy actually goes

Ideal model: PE = mgh. For our 5g marble from 45cm, that's about 0.022 J of stored energy. None of it comes back: paper friction, tape seams and air resistance eat roughly a quarter of it by loop #1, and most of the rest across the funnels. That's why funnel exits are slow and the banana catch is gentle — by the finish line, the marble is nearly out of energy. Which is exactly what a catch basin wants.
